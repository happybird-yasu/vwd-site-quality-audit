# Population Flow Map: Chubu and Hokuriku (2019, 2021 and 2025)

Chubu and Hokuriku lie in the center of Honshu, Japan's largest island, stretching from the Pacific side to the Sea of Japan side. On this site, six prefectures are presented together: Toyama, Ishikawa, Fukui, Gifu, Shizuoka and Aichi.

City streets lined with office towers in Nagoya and towns full of car-related factories; mountain villages and tea fields; harbor towns on the Sea of Japan and snow-covered mountains. In Chubu and Hokuriku, manufacturing towns and rich nature sit side by side, and there are many different ways of life.

People who live in towns and farming villages like these also move for reasons such as school, a new job, or family life.

In each of these towns and cities, where do people come from, and where do they go when they leave?

Why not trace the flows of people in each part of Chubu and Hokuriku by following the map?

## Why We Made the Chubu/Hokuriku Edition

“Are people from all over Chubu and Hokuriku gathering in Tokyo, Nagoya and Kanazawa?” That question made us want to look more closely at the movement of people within Chubu and Hokuriku.

The Chubu/Hokuriku edition looks at individual municipalities and lets you compare 2019, 2021 and 2025.

I also wanted to know what kinds of people are moving, so the map can also be switched by sex, nationality and age group.

## What I Noticed on the Map

What stood out to me on the map of Chubu and Hokuriku was that Aichi Prefecture, where manufacturing is thriving, had net outflow in all three years: 2019, 2021 and 2025.

The impression that people gather where industry is strong may not be enough to capture how people move.

That said, a net outflow for the whole prefecture does not mean that people are leaving every town in it. Looking at individual municipalities also reveals movements that differ from the prefecture as a whole.

The map also shows connections between places that the picture of everyone gathering in Tokyo cannot capture.

Following the flows toward large cities and the moves between nearby towns, I was reminded that even within Chubu and Hokuriku there are many different movements of people.

In your country, what kinds of movements are there in towns full of factories and in the towns around them?

【Net Migration Data Card: Chubu and Hokuriku】

Net migration is the number of people who moved in minus the number who moved out. A positive figure means more people moved in than out (net inflow); a negative figure means more moved out than in (net outflow).

## About the Data

The totals and net migration figures shown here cover domestic moves by Japanese nationals and foreign nationals combined.

The map's age view uses four groups: 0–19, 20–39, 40–59 and 60 and over. People whose age is unknown are not included in these four groups.

This page is based on data published by the Statistics Bureau of Japan in its “Report on Internal Migration in Japan.” Visible World by Data compiled and tabulated the data and turned it into the map and the data card.

The years covered are 2019, 2021 and 2025. The Chubu/Hokuriku edition of this site covers six prefectures: Toyama, Ishikawa, Fukui, Gifu, Shizuoka and Aichi.

The migration shown here means people who changed their registered address across municipal or similar boundaries within Japan. It does not show people entering Japan from abroad or leaving Japan for other countries.

It also differs from commuting, travel or tourist crowds, and from population change that includes births and deaths. If the same person moved more than once during the period, each move is counted.

The municipality-level data on the map include moves within the same prefecture. In some large cities known as designated cities, moves to a different ward within the same city are also counted.

The data card adds up the net migration of the six covered prefectures. Moves among the six prefectures cancel out to zero for the region as a whole, so the card shows the difference between moves in and out relative to places outside the region. This is a different measure from the municipal in-migration figures shown on the map or from the sum of the rankings.

### Notes on Reading the Figures

- Hamamatsu is tabulated by its seven former wards in 2019 and 2021, and by its three new wards (Chuo, Hamana and Tenryu) in 2025. Because the ward areas differ, figures for each ward cannot be compared directly across years.
- “What I Noticed on the Map” records impressions from looking at the map and the figures. It is not a statistically verified conclusion about the reasons for moving or about regional trends.
- Migration alone cannot tell you whether a region's total population went up or down.
- The rankings cover the municipalities and wards listed individually in the official table. Areas grouped under “others” and similar rows are not ranked, so this is not a complete ranking of all municipalities.
- The total shown is the official table's total, not the sum of the top-10 ranking.
- The nationality switch shows people who moved within Japan, by nationality. It does not show movement across national borders.
- Where municipal boundaries or classifications have changed, comparisons across years need care.
