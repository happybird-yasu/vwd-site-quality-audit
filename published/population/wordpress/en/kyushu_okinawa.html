Kyushu is one of the four main islands of Japan and lies in the southwest of the country. Okinawa is a group of islands that continues further southwest from Kyushu. On this site, the seven prefectures of Kyushu and Okinawa Prefecture are presented together.

City streets lined with office towers in Fukuoka, the grasslands of Aso, towns with a view of Sakurajima; hot-spring villages in the mountains and harbor towns by the sea; and the blue sea and coral reefs of Okinawa. Kyushu and Okinawa have many different ways of life, each with its own nature and culture.

People who live in towns and on islands like these also move for reasons such as school, a new job, or family life.

In each of these towns and cities, where do people come from, and where do they go when they leave?

Why not trace the flows of people in each part of Kyushu and Okinawa by following the map?

Why We Made the Kyushu/Okinawa Edition

“Are people from all over Kyushu and Okinawa gathering in Tokyo and Fukuoka?” That question made us want to look more closely at the movement of people within Kyushu and Okinawa.

The Kyushu/Okinawa edition looks at individual municipalities and lets you compare 2019, 2021 and 2025.

What I Noticed on the Map

What stood out to me on the map of Kyushu and Okinawa was that the flow of people toward Fukuoka Prefecture looked large.

I had also assumed that Okinawa Prefecture had net outflow, but in 2019 it had net inflow. In 2021 and 2025, by contrast, it had net outflow, and it stayed with me that the picture differs from year to year.

I was also struck that even on remote islands, there was more movement of people than I had expected.

Following the moves between towns on the Kyushu mainland and the connections with islands across the sea, you can see many different comings and goings.

Net Migration for the Whole of Kyushu and Okinawa

How has the difference between people moving in and people moving out changed across the whole of Kyushu and Okinawa?

The data card below compares net migration for 2019, 2021 and 2025.

Net migration is the number of people who moved in minus the number who moved out. A positive figure means more people moved in than out (net inflow); a negative figure means more moved out than in (net outflow).

About the Data

The totals and net migration figures shown here cover domestic moves by Japanese nationals and foreign nationals combined.

The map's age view uses four groups: 0–19, 20–39, 40–59 and 60 and over. People whose age is unknown are not included in these four groups.

This page is based on data published by the Statistics Bureau of Japan in its “Report on Internal Migration in Japan.” Visible World by Data compiled and tabulated the data and turned it into the map and the data card.

The years covered are 2019, 2021 and 2025. The Kyushu/Okinawa edition of this site covers eight prefectures: Fukuoka, Saga, Nagasaki, Kumamoto, Oita, Miyazaki, Kagoshima and Okinawa.

The migration shown here means people who changed their registered address across municipal or similar boundaries within Japan. It does not show people entering Japan from abroad or leaving Japan for other countries.

It also differs from commuting, travel or tourist crowds, and from population change that includes births and deaths. If the same person moved more than once during the period, each move is counted.

The municipality-level data on the map include moves within the same prefecture. In some large cities known as designated cities, moves to a different ward within the same city are also counted.

The data card adds up the net migration of the eight covered prefectures. Moves among the eight prefectures cancel out to zero for the region as a whole, so the card shows the difference between moves in and out relative to places outside the region. This is a different measure from the municipal in-migration figures shown on the map or from the sum of the rankings.

Notes on Reading the Figures

  • “What I Noticed on the Map” records impressions from looking at the map and the figures. It is not a statistically verified conclusion about the reasons for moving or about regional trends.
  • Migration alone cannot tell you whether a region's total population went up or down.
  • The rankings cover the municipalities and wards listed individually in the official table. Areas grouped under “others” and similar rows are not ranked, so this is not a complete ranking of all municipalities.
  • The total shown is the official table's total, not the sum of the top-10 ranking.
  • The nationality switch shows people who moved within Japan, by nationality. It does not show movement across national borders.
  • Where municipal boundaries or classifications have changed, comparisons across years need care.
