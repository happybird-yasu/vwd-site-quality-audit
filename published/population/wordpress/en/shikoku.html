Shikoku is one of the four main islands of Japan, lying south of Honshu and east of Kyushu. It consists of four prefectures — Tokushima, Kagawa, Ehime and Kochi — and is connected to Honshu by bridges across the Seto Inland Sea.

Islands scattered across the Seto Inland Sea and coastline facing the Pacific; the deep Shikoku Mountains, riverside villages, and the pilgrimage paths of the ohenro winding through farmland. The ohenro is a pilgrimage to 88 temples around Shikoku.

People who live in towns and farming villages surrounded by sea and mountains like these also move for reasons such as school, a new job, or family life.

In each of these towns and cities, where do people come from, and where do they go when they leave?

Why not trace the flows of people in each part of Shikoku by following the map?

Why We Made the Shikoku Edition

“Since Shikoku and Honshu were connected by bridges, are people moving out to Honshu?” That question made us want to look more closely at the movement of people within Shikoku.

The Shikoku edition looks at individual municipalities and lets you compare 2019, 2021 and 2025.

I also wanted to know what kinds of people are moving, so the map can also be switched by sex, nationality and age group.

Exploring Population Flow in Shikoku on the Map

This map lets you compare internal migration in Shikoku across 2019, 2021 and 2025.

Switch between sex, nationality and age group to see where people moved in from and moved out to, municipality by municipality. “Moving in” means changing your registered address to that area; “moving out” means changing it from that area to another area.

You don't need to know any place names in Shikoku. Just pick a spot on the map that catches your eye and follow the numbers to see where people came from and where they moved on to.

What I Noticed on the Map

What stood out to me on the map of Shikoku was that there seemed to be many connections with Honshu as well as within Shikoku.

I noticed not only people leaving for Honshu, but also people coming from Honshu to Shikoku and people moving between towns within Shikoku.

Following the connections with areas across the sea and the moves between nearby towns reveals movements of people that a one-way picture cannot show.

Net Migration for the Whole of Shikoku

How has the difference between people moving in and people moving out changed across the whole of Shikoku?

The data card below compares net migration for 2019, 2021 and 2025.

Net migration is the number of people who moved in minus the number who moved out. A positive figure means more people moved in than out (net inflow); a negative figure means more moved out than in (net outflow).

About the Data

The totals and net migration figures shown here cover domestic moves by Japanese nationals and foreign nationals combined.

The map's age view uses four groups: 0–19, 20–39, 40–59 and 60 and over. People whose age is unknown are not included in these four groups.

This page is based on data published by the Statistics Bureau of Japan in its “Report on Internal Migration in Japan.” Visible World by Data compiled and tabulated the data and turned it into the map and the data card.

The years covered are 2019, 2021 and 2025. The Shikoku edition of this site covers four prefectures: Tokushima, Kagawa, Ehime and Kochi.

The migration shown here means people who changed their registered address across municipal or similar boundaries within Japan. It does not show people entering Japan from abroad or leaving Japan for other countries.

It also differs from commuting, travel or tourist crowds, and from population change that includes births and deaths. If the same person moved more than once during the period, each move is counted.

The municipality-level data on the map include moves within the same prefecture.

The data card adds up the net migration of the four covered prefectures. Moves among the four prefectures cancel out to zero for the region as a whole, so the card shows the difference between moves in and out relative to places outside the region. This is a different measure from the municipal in-migration figures shown on the map or from the sum of the rankings.

Notes on Reading the Figures

  • “What I Noticed on the Map” records impressions from looking at the map and the figures. It is not a statistically verified conclusion about the reasons for moving or about regional trends.
  • This map alone cannot tell you what effect the bridges had on people's movements.
  • Migration alone cannot tell you whether a region's total population went up or down.
  • The rankings cover the municipalities and wards listed individually in the official table. Areas grouped under “others” and similar rows are not ranked, so this is not a complete ranking of all municipalities.
  • The total shown is the official table's total, not the sum of the top-10 ranking.
  • The nationality switch shows people who moved within Japan, by nationality. It does not show movement across national borders.
  • Where municipal boundaries or classifications have changed, comparisons across years need care.
